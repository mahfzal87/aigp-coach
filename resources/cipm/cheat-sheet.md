# CIPM revision cheat sheet

An original operating guide for privacy-management revision. The structure below is our teaching aid, not a reproduction of IAPP’s exam domains. Use the current official Body of Knowledge to check the rest of your preparation.

## 1. Give the programme a mandate

Start with what the organisation does, which people it affects and which privacy obligations and risks arise. Define the programme’s objectives, scope, leadership support and decision rights. A broad policy without an accountable owner is difficult to operate.

A programme charter can describe authority, responsibilities, reporting lines, resources and review arrangements. The useful question is whether teams know when to involve privacy and who can resolve a disagreement.

- Who sponsors the programme?
- Who owns decisions and who advises?
- Which activities and entities are in scope?
- What happens when business pressure conflicts with a privacy control?

## 2. Discover how data actually moves

Build and maintain an inventory of relevant processing: purposes, data, people, systems, recipients, locations, retention and ownership. Validate it with the teams that operate the processes. A spreadsheet created once is not a reliable picture of a changing business.

Fictional example: Alderbridge Services records its customer portal but misses the weekly export sent to a support supplier. The missing transfer matters even if the central application has strong security.

- Trace collection, use, sharing and disposal.
- Include vendors, exports and manual workarounds.
- Assign change triggers so records are updated when the business changes.

## 3. Prioritise risks, then choose controls

Assess consequences for people alongside organisational exposure. Use a consistent method and document assumptions. Prioritisation should connect severity, likelihood, existing measures and uncertainty rather than simply counting open issues.

Each treatment needs an owner, due date and evidence of effectiveness. Residual risk acceptance is a decision by someone with the right authority; it is not the default result of missing a deadline.

- Avoid treating a control’s existence as proof of effectiveness.
- Connect privacy reviews to procurement and product changes.
- Record unresolved risks and conditions of approval.

## 4. Make the everyday workflows usable

Rights requests, notices, consent where relevant, retention, supplier oversight and incident handling need repeatable workflows. Define intake, validation, responsible teams, escalation and closure evidence. Legal requirements differ by jurisdiction and processing context.

A workflow should survive absence or staff turnover. If only one person knows which system contains a requested record, the programme has a dependency to address.

- Can frontline staff recognise and route a request?
- Can teams find the relevant records and recipients?
- Can a vendor meet the agreed assistance obligations?
- Can the organisation demonstrate what action it took?

## 5. Measure whether the programme works

Pair activity measures with measures of timeliness, coverage and quality. Training completion tells you who finished a module, not whether people can identify a risky practice. A low incident count may reflect good controls or poor reporting.

Use trends and denominators. “Eight overdue reviews” means something different if there are ten reviews in scope rather than eight hundred. Include context, exceptions and the decision you want the reader to make.

- Activity: what work was completed?
- Coverage: how much of the relevant population was reached?
- Effectiveness: what evidence shows the control works?
- Action: what changes because of the metric?

## 6. Test, learn and improve

Run practical exercises, sample control operation and examine incidents for causes and corrective actions. Improvement closes the loop: change the process, assign ownership and check whether the fix worked.

For study, take one fictional launch and follow it through intake, assessment, approval, operation, incident and retirement. Explain each handoff without relying on a memorised list. That exposes gaps a vocabulary quiz can miss.
