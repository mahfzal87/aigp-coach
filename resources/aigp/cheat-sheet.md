# AIGP revision cheat sheet

Use this revision sheet to retrieve concepts from memory, then apply them to a fictional system. It is a selective learning aid, not an official syllabus, legal checklist or prediction of your exam result.

## 1. Start with the decision, not the model

Describe the system in one sentence: what it does, for whom, using what information, and with what consequence. A language model drafting a lunch menu and the same model ranking job applicants do not present the same governance problem.

Then identify people affected, the intended purpose, the deployment environment and limits on use. A benchmark score cannot answer all of those questions.

- Purpose: which task is the system intended to support?
- Context: where will it run and who could be affected?
- Authority: may it suggest, decide or act?
- Evidence: what would justify allowing that level of authority?

## 2. Separate the layers of responsibility

A model is a component. A system combines components, interfaces, data and operating arrangements. An organisation decides how that system is used. A strong component does not automatically make the complete deployment safe.

Under the EU AI Act, provider and deployer are legal roles tied to the facts. Developing a system or having it developed and placing it on the market or putting it into service under your name can create provider responsibilities. Using a system under your authority can make you a deployer. Certain modifications or changes of purpose can shift responsibilities.

- Do not assume the purchaser is always only a deployer.
- Do not substitute a contract label for the legal definition.
- Check the relevant provision and applicability dates before making a real compliance decision.

## 3. Keep four risk-management questions separate

The NIST AI RMF organises its core around Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. Treat them as connected functions, not a four-step project plan that ends at launch. Governance spans the other work.

Our study prompt: who owns the decision; what context creates risk; what evidence characterises it; what response is appropriate? Testing belongs inside this wider process.

- Govern: policies, roles, culture and accountability.
- Map: purpose, context, affected parties and possible impacts.
- Measure: assess and monitor relevant risks with suitable methods.
- Manage: prioritise and respond to risks, including deciding not to proceed.

## 4. Learn the pairs that look interchangeable

Accuracy describes performance against a chosen target. Fairness requires deciding which treatment or outcome concerns matter, then examining them in context. A high average score can conceal poor performance for a group.

Transparency concerns making relevant information available. Explainability concerns understanding an output or mechanism. Human oversight needs a person with time, information and authority to intervene; merely displaying an approval button is not enough.

- Validation asks whether the system is suitable for its intended use.
- Monitoring asks whether its behaviour and context continue to justify use.
- An explanation is evidence to assess, not proof that an output is correct.

## 5. Follow the whole lifecycle

Before deployment, record requirements, data provenance, evaluation evidence, limitations and approval conditions. During operation, monitor relevant signals, investigate incidents and reassess changes. At retirement, address access, dependencies, retained data and records.

Fictional example: Solmere uses a forecasting tool to schedule deliveries. A new region has different weather patterns. The responsible response is to assess the changed context and evidence, not assume the launch approval covers every future region.

- What change triggers reassessment?
- Who can pause the system?
- How are affected people informed or supported?
- What evidence is retained to explain a decision later?

## 6. Use a framework without confusing it with a law

A law creates legal obligations within its scope. A voluntary risk framework helps organise risk work. A management system standard sets requirements for an organisation’s management arrangements. They may support one another, but none is a universal substitute for the others.

For revision, explain the difference aloud before consulting your notes. If you can name a document but cannot explain the decision it helps someone make, return to an example. Always use IAPP’s current official resources to check your study coverage.
