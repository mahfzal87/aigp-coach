# AIGP glossary

## AI system

A system that uses machine-based inference to produce outputs such as predictions, recommendations or content. Legal definitions vary; identify the relevant instrument before applying a definition.

**Example:** A tool combines a model, applicant data and a ranking interface for recruiters.

**Keep separate:** An AI model is a component; the complete system includes its operating context.

## Intended purpose

The use for which a system is designed or supplied, including relevant conditions and limitations. It anchors the question of whether evidence is suitable for a proposed deployment.

**Example:** A tool validated to summarise internal manuals is proposed for customer eligibility decisions.

**Keep separate:** Actual use can depart from intended purpose and require reassessment.

## Provider

Under the EU AI Act, a role that can arise when an entity develops, or has developed, an AI system and markets or puts it into service under its name or trademark. The definition also covers placing general-purpose AI models on the market. Check the full definition and specific facts.

**Example:** A firm commissions an AI system and releases it under its own brand.

**Keep separate:** The organisation writing the code is not necessarily the only possible provider.

## Deployer

Under the EU AI Act, an entity using an AI system under its authority, with an exception for personal non-professional activity. The role carries context-dependent duties.

**Example:** An employer uses a supplier’s AI system within its recruitment process.

**Keep separate:** Buying software does not determine every responsibility; changes can affect the role.

## AI governance

The decisions, responsibilities and operating arrangements used to direct and oversee AI development and use. It connects organisational objectives with risks, obligations and accountability.

**Example:** A launch review specifies evidence, permitted uses and who can suspend deployment.

**Keep separate:** A committee alone is not an operating governance process.

## Risk assessment

A structured examination of possible adverse consequences and the conditions that make them more or less likely or severe. Methods should fit the use and available evidence.

**Example:** A team examines whether inaccurate recommendations could deny customers an essential service.

**Keep separate:** A list of model defects is narrower than an assessment of downstream impacts.

## Impact assessment

An examination of how a system or activity may affect people, groups or other interests. Different legal and organisational assessments have different scopes and triggers.

**Example:** A team studies how a new screening tool may affect applicants with disabilities.

**Keep separate:** An assessment is not automatically a regulatory certification or permission to launch.

## Data provenance

Information about where data came from and how it was collected, transformed and used. Provenance supports quality, rights and accountability checks.

**Example:** A dataset record explains its source, collection conditions and transformations.

**Keep separate:** Knowing a download URL does not establish permission to use the data.

## Training data

Data used to adjust a model’s learned parameters during training. Its suitability depends on the task and intended population, among other factors.

**Example:** Historical labelled examples teach a model a classification task.

**Keep separate:** Evaluation data should test performance without simply rewarding memorisation of training examples.

## Validation

Evidence-based checking that a system is suitable for its intended purpose and conditions of use. It involves more than achieving a single technical benchmark.

**Example:** A team tests a workflow with representative users and realistic operating conditions.

**Keep separate:** Verification checks specified requirements; validation asks whether the intended use is adequately served.

## Monitoring

Ongoing observation of system behaviour and operating conditions to detect changes or problems that need action. Useful monitoring connects signals to owners and response thresholds.

**Example:** An owner receives an alert when error rates rise in a new region.

**Keep separate:** Logging collects records; monitoring interprets signals and supports action.

## Data drift

A change in the distribution of input data relative to an earlier reference. It can reduce relevance of previous evaluation results, but does not by itself prove harm or performance failure.

**Example:** The language mix of customer enquiries changes after expansion.

**Keep separate:** Concept drift concerns changes in the relationship being modelled.

## Explainability

The ability to provide understandable information about a model’s mechanism or the basis of an output. The appropriate explanation depends on its audience and purpose.

**Example:** An analyst needs actionable reasons to investigate a flagged transaction.

**Keep separate:** An understandable explanation can still describe an incorrect result.

## Transparency

Providing relevant information about a system, its use or its limitations to appropriate audiences. More information is not automatically more useful information.

**Example:** Users are told when they are interacting with an AI assistant and how to seek help.

**Keep separate:** Transparency is broader than explaining one prediction.

## Human oversight

Arrangements that enable people to understand, supervise and, where appropriate, intervene in a system’s operation. Effective oversight requires capability and practical authority.

**Example:** A reviewer can reject a recommendation and pause the workflow when a pattern of error appears.

**Keep separate:** A person clicking approve without sufficient information is not meaningful oversight.

## Automation bias

A tendency to rely excessively on automated recommendations, including when other evidence should prompt doubt. Workflow design and training can affect this risk.

**Example:** A reviewer accepts an implausible recommendation because the interface labels it high confidence.

**Keep separate:** Confidence displays are not an assurance that the recommendation is correct.

## Residual risk

Risk that remains after selected controls are implemented. Acceptance should be made by an authorised owner with evidence, limits and review conditions.

**Example:** A system is permitted for low-impact suggestions while unresolved errors rule out automated decisions.

**Keep separate:** A control being installed does not mean all associated risk has disappeared.

## AI management system

An organisation’s coordinated arrangements for governing and improving its AI activities. ISO/IEC 42001 specifies requirements for this type of management system.

**Example:** Leadership reviews AI objectives, responsibilities and corrective actions on an ongoing basis.

**Keep separate:** Management-system certification is not a guarantee that every model output is safe or correct.
