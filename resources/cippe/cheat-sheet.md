# CIPP/E revision cheat sheet

A selective GDPR-centred revision aid for CIPP/E learners. European privacy includes other instruments and national contexts: this sheet is not the complete exam syllabus or advice for a specific processing activity.

## 1. Establish the facts before choosing a rule

Ask what information is processed, why, by whom and in which context. Personal data is not limited to names or obviously sensitive records. Information can relate to an identifiable person through combinations of data or indirect identifiers.

Scope matters. Do not assume that an organisation being outside the EU places all its processing outside the GDPR, or that every activity involving an EU resident automatically falls within it. Apply the territorial-scope tests to the actual activity.

- What is the specific processing purpose?
- Which people and data are involved?
- Who determines the purpose and essential means?
- Which legal instruments and territorial rules apply?

## 2. Separate controller, processor and joint controller

A controller determines purposes and means. A processor handles personal data on behalf of a controller. Joint control can arise where parties jointly determine purposes and means. Contract labels matter as evidence, but cannot override what the parties actually decide and do.

Fictional example: Bellhaven Retail instructs a payroll supplier to process employee pay. That activity may make the supplier a processor. If the supplier independently uses the records to develop its own marketing profiles, that separate activity needs a fresh role analysis.

- The same organisation can have different roles for different activities.
- A processor may make practical technical decisions without necessarily determining the essential means.
- Outsourcing does not remove the controller’s responsibilities.

## 3. Choose a lawful basis for each purpose

Article 6 provides six lawful bases: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task and legitimate interests. Availability depends on the facts and the conditions of each basis. A convenient label is not a legal analysis.

Consent must meet its requirements, including a genuine choice and the ability to withdraw. Contract necessity does not cover every activity mentioned in terms. Legitimate interests requires an interest, necessity and balancing assessment; it is not a catch-all. Special-category data also requires an applicable Article 9 condition.

- Decide and document the basis before processing.
- Keep purpose, necessity and proportionality visible in the reasoning.
- Check whether another rule imposes additional conditions.

## 4. Turn the principles into operating questions

Lawfulness, fairness and transparency concern whether processing is justified and understandable. Purpose limitation and data minimisation concern what the organisation is trying to achieve and the information actually needed. Accuracy, storage limitation and security require continuing attention.

Accountability means being responsible for compliance and able to demonstrate it. A policy statement is one piece of evidence, not the whole answer.

- Can the purpose be achieved with less data?
- Who corrects inaccurate records?
- What retention rule is implemented in the systems?
- What evidence shows that the controls operate?

## 5. Match the right to its conditions

Access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability and objection are distinct rights with different conditions and exceptions. Do not translate every request into “delete everything”. Identify the right being exercised and the processing at issue.

Portability applies under defined conditions, including relevant consent- or contract-based automated processing. Erasure has exceptions. Article 22 addresses certain solely automated decisions producing legal or similarly significant effects; it is not a universal prohibition on using automation.

- Verify identity proportionately, without collecting unnecessary extra information.
- Record the request and route it to the responsible owner.
- Check the applicable timing, exceptions and response requirements.

## 6. Recognise the additional assessments

A DPIA is required where processing is likely to result in a high risk to people’s rights and freedoms, taking the relevant factors into account. It is performed before the processing, not only after a complaint. A generic security review is not automatically a sufficient DPIA.

A personal data breach can involve confidentiality, integrity or availability. Notification decisions require the relevant risk analysis. International transfers need their own Chapter V analysis; a lawful basis for collection is not itself a transfer mechanism.

- Separate the underlying processing basis from a transfer safeguard.
- Document breach facts and decisions, including where notification is not required.
- Return to the relevant legal text and regulator guidance when a threshold determines the answer.
