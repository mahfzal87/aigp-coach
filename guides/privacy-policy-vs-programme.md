# A privacy policy is not a privacy programme

Turn promises into owners, workflows and evidence. A practical starting point for studying privacy programme management.

## Read the promise, then find the process

A privacy notice tells people relevant information about processing. An internal policy gives organisational direction. A programme connects responsibilities, decisions and everyday work so those statements can be carried out. None of these artefacts should be mistaken for all the others.

Take a simple promise: “We keep personal information only as long as needed.” The sentence is easy to write. The operating questions are harder: needed for which purpose, in which system, until which event, under whose authority, and with what evidence of disposal?

## Start with an owner and an inventory

Fictional Westhaven Travel introduces a privacy programme after a period of growth. Its first useful task is not to create a fifty-page policy. It needs to understand its processing and establish who can make and implement decisions across booking, marketing, suppliers and support.

An inventory gives the programme something concrete to work with. It should be maintained through changes, not treated as a one-time discovery exercise. Assigning a purpose and owner to an activity makes it easier to identify which obligations, controls and reviews belong to it.

## Build workflows around real handoffs

A rights request may arrive through a support agent. A vendor change may begin in procurement. An incident may be discovered by engineering. Each needs a route into the programme with a clear owner, relevant information and escalation conditions.

A procedure that requires everyone to recognise legal terminology is fragile. Staff should know practical signals: a person asking for their records, a new supplier receiving customer data, an unexpected disclosure. The programme then supplies the specialist analysis and coordination needed to respond.

## Collect evidence that a control operates

Suppose the retention policy is approved and training is complete. Ask for evidence that expired records were handled correctly, that exceptions were justified and that the responsible teams can explain the process. Those checks test more than the existence of a document.

Evidence can include samples, system behaviour, decision records and incident learning. The appropriate method depends on the control. A conversation about what “usually happens” may identify a process, but it is weaker than demonstrating what happened in a relevant case.

## Use a programme question in every revision session

Pick one privacy obligation and trace it through the organisation. Who sets the requirement? Who implements it? How is it checked? What happens when it fails? Who can authorise an exception, and when is the decision revisited?

This is an original study exercise rather than a reproduction of IAPP’s domains. It helps distinguish awareness of a privacy concept from understanding how to manage it over time. Use the official current CIPM resources to ensure your preparation covers the complete scope.

- Promise: what should happen?
- Owner: who is accountable for making it happen?
- Workflow: how does it happen in practice?
- Evidence: how do we know it happened?
- Improvement: what changes when it did not?
