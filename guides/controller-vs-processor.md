# Controller or processor? Follow the decisions, not the contract label

Three fictional data-processing relationships show why the same supplier can have different roles for different activities.

## The first question is “for what purpose?”

A controller determines the purposes and means of processing. A processor processes personal data on behalf of a controller. These summaries are easy to recall; applying them means examining the specific activity and who makes its essential decisions.

Do not classify an entire organisation once and then apply that label to everything it does. A supplier may process a client’s records on instructions while acting as a controller for its own employment records. “Technology company” and “customer” are commercial descriptions, not GDPR roles.

## A supplier following instructions

Fictional Larchstone Retail decides why it needs employee payroll processing and sets the relevant requirements. It hires a supplier to perform that processing under instructions. The supplier chooses practical technical arrangements to run the service.

Those facts can support a controller–processor relationship. A processor does not need to be a passive machine with no technical discretion. The distinction includes the nature of the decisions: deciding practical implementation details is not automatically the same as determining the purpose or essential means. The arrangement still needs the applicable legal and contractual safeguards.

## Independent reuse is a separate activity

Now suppose the supplier decides to take payroll data and use it for its own unrelated market research. That is not simply another technical choice made to deliver payroll. The supplier has introduced a separate purpose.

Reassess the role and lawfulness for that processing. Calling the supplier a processor in the original agreement does not make every later use processing on behalf of the customer. The exercise is to locate the changed fact and analyse the new activity, not to rely on the most convenient label.

## When two parties design the purpose together

Fictional Elmford Association and a research partner jointly design a member study, deciding why the data will be collected and the essential features of its use. Depending on the full facts, that may create joint control.

Do not jump to joint control whenever data is exchanged. Separate controllers can also share information. Joint control depends on joint determination, which may involve converging decisions rather than identical influence over every detail. The parties must address their relevant responsibilities without preventing individuals from exercising their rights.

## A method for difficult scenarios

List each processing activity separately. For each, write its purpose, the key decisions and who makes them. Compare the written arrangement with the real practice. Then consider the obligations arising from the role, rather than using the desired obligations to choose the role.

In a real project, review the full EDPB guidance and applicable facts. These examples are deliberately simplified to teach the distinction. They do not establish that every payroll service, research collaboration or data-sharing arrangement has the same role allocation.

- Purpose: who decides why this activity happens?
- Essential means: who determines the important features of the processing?
- Instructions: is the supplier acting on behalf of another party?
- Reality check: has either party introduced an independent purpose?
