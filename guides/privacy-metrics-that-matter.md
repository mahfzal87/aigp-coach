# Your privacy dashboard is green. What does it actually prove?

Training counts and closed tickets can hide the problem. Learn to connect a metric to a decision, its denominator and its limitations.

## A metric is useful when it changes a decision

A green dashboard can mean good performance. It can also mean that the measures are incomplete, thresholds are too easy or problems are not being reported. The first question should be what a measure tells its audience to do.

These examples are practical programme-management suggestions, not mandatory regulatory metrics. Select measures that fit the organisation’s activities, risks and objectives, then record their limitations. A measure becomes less useful when its meaning is assumed rather than explained.

## Training completion measures activity

Fictional Silvergrove Media reports that nearly everyone has completed privacy training. That is useful coverage information. It does not establish whether a support agent can recognise a rights request or whether procurement staff involve privacy before sharing data with a supplier.

Pair the completion count with suitable evidence of application: a short role-specific exercise, review of actual handoffs or recurring incident themes. Do not turn the exercise into a claim that a quiz perfectly predicts behaviour. Use it to identify where support or process changes are needed.

## Counts need denominators and context

“Five reviews are overdue” tells you less than “five of twelve high-priority reviews are overdue, and two affect launches this week”. The second statement helps the owner understand the coverage and consequence.

The denominator itself needs care. If the organisation has not identified all relevant vendors or processing activities, a high percentage of completed reviews can overstate programme coverage. Define the population, record exclusions and track whether the inventory is sufficiently reliable for the decision.

## Speed can conflict with quality

A team rewarded only for closing requests quickly may close incomplete cases or avoid difficult ones. Pair response-time measures with quality checks and the relevant legal requirements. A fast answer is not necessarily a correct or complete answer.

Similarly, a falling incident count is ambiguous. It may reflect better controls, or staff may have stopped reporting. Look at reporting routes, near misses, time to detection and lessons from investigated events. Explain the uncertainty instead of choosing the most flattering interpretation.

## Build a small, actionable set

Choose a few measures that support real oversight: coverage of high-risk activity reviews, ageing of important corrective actions, sampled control effectiveness and recurring causes of failure. For each, name the owner, data source, review frequency and action threshold.

Review the measures themselves. If a dashboard number never changes a decision, ask whether it is needed. If teams can make the number look better while the underlying risk grows, redesign the measure. In study scenarios, this is often the difference between describing activity and demonstrating programme effectiveness.

- What objective does the measure support?
- What population and period does it cover?
- What can it not establish?
- Who acts on an adverse signal?
- What evidence shows the response improved the situation?
